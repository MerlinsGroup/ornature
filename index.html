The Detox Collection

Six loose leaf blends in glass, in a box you keep. We took the decision early not to sell tea bags: the leaf is bigger, the infusion is cleaner, and there is no bleached paper or staple to throw away afterwards.

Every box ships with a strainer, and the counter sells the pot and the cup to go with it, so nobody has to work out the kit themselves.

First off the line

The one we made first

This is the first OrNature product to come back from the filler, photographed on a pub table twenty minutes after we opened the box. Two hundred grams of lion’s mane, reishi and chaga, with cacao and turmeric to make it drinkable without milk.

We checked the fill weight, the seal and the print, then drank one. The label has had two small changes since.

The shop

A juice bar at the front, a food counter down the middle, and the grocery range on the wall behind it.

One room doing three jobs. You can come in for a coffee and leave in four minutes, sit down for a bowl at lunch, or fill a bag with the week’s milk, muesli and honey on the way home. The point of putting all three under one roof is that the second visit costs the customer nothing to make.

Field notes

What we are learning while we pick a site. Written up as we go, numbers and all.

We would rather show the working than publish a launch date. These are the notes behind the decision on where the first shop goes, who supplies it and what has to be in the cabinet on day one.

Note 01 · Long Street

Counting covers outside somebody else’s shop

Darren Leech, our director, spent a weekday morning on Long Street with a notebook. The bakery he is standing in front of has been trading since 1954 and every pavement table was taken before eleven.

That answers the question we actually needed answering. A Cotswolds high street will carry a full price food counter on an ordinary Tuesday. The opening is not whether people will stop, it is what is in the cabinet when they do: almost nobody here is selling raw juice, live yoghurt and a grocery shelf from the same till.

Note 02 · Which town

Resident base first, visitors second

The pretty towns are not the strong ones. Stow-on-the-Wold and Moreton-in-Marsh trade hard in August and go quiet in February, which is no use to a shop selling milk and juice on a weekly repeat.

So we ranked the shortlist on year-round trade rather than footfall, and weighted permanent residents over visitor spend. Witney is the largest town on the list at about 31,200 people; Cirencester follows at 20,229 and is the largest town inside the Cotswolds proper. Stroud, at 13,468 in the parish, sits inside a much bigger connected urban area, which makes it commercially stronger than the raw number suggests. It was named one of the best places to live in the UK for 2026 by The Times.

Note 03 · Supply

Four suppliers, so a bad week is never a closed counter

A juice bar lives or dies on produce arriving. One organic wholesaler covers the daily core and the fruit we cannot grow here; a local organic farm covers seasonal veg, herbs and soft fruit; a third sits behind both as backup; and a Cheltenham wholesaler is close enough for a same-day run when something fails.

On the claims side we have made one deliberate edit. We do not say “non-toxic fruit and veg”, because organic certification restricts synthetic pesticides rather than proving their absence. What we will say is that everything is made daily from certified organic fruit and vegetables, carefully sourced for quality and traceability, with batch traceability and periodic independent residue testing behind it.

How it gets made

Four steps between the field and the fridge, and nothing in between that we cannot show you.

Grown

Certified organic growers, most of them close enough to visit in a morning. Seasonal lines change when the field does.

Pressed

Cold pressed and bottled in small batches. No pasteurising, no concentrate, no water added to stretch it.

Tested

Batch traceability on every run, with periodic independent residue and microbiological testing. Certificates on request.

Returned

Glass over plastic wherever it works. Bring the milk bottle and the tea caddy back and we will refill them.

Glass, not plastic

The packaging decision

Glass costs more to buy, more to ship and more to break. We took it anyway on the lines where it changes the product: water, milk, juice and the tea caddies. Everything dry goes into kraft pouches with a window, because a pouch that shows the contents sells better than a printed picture of them.
